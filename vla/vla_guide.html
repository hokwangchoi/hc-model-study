VLA — Vision-Language-Action

π0-style: one transformer with two sets of weights — a pretrained VLM for images and text, and a smaller "action expert" for robot state and actions — sharing attention layer by layer, and generating continuous action chunks with flow matching.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Transformer: attention masks, KV cache
  • Vision-Language Models: image tokens + text tokens in one LLM
  • Diffusion (DiT): generating by iterative denoising
  • Mixture-of-Experts helps: π0 routes tokens to one of two weight sets

Time: about 60–75 min to read, plus 20 min for vla.py and the exercises.

What you'll learn

  • Why robots predict chunks of actions, and how often π0 re-plans
  • How π0 joins a VLM and an action expert with one block-causal attention, and why that makes a prefix KV cache possible
  • Flow matching from scratch: the straight-line training target, Euler sampling, and the time/sign conventions that differ between papers and code
  • A roofline view of VLA inference on Orin and Thor: the VLM prefix is compute-heavy, the 10 Euler steps are memory-bound
  • What came after π0, as of Oct 2026

The Core Idea: Instruction In, Action Chunk Out

A VLA takes what the robot sees (cameras), what it has been asked to do (language) and where it currently is (proprioception), and outputs a sequence of future commands — an action chunk. π0 predicts H = 50 actions at a time, i.e. one second of motion for a robot controlled at 50 Hz.

Why chunks? Predicting one action per observation would mean running a 3.3B-parameter model 50 times per second. Chunks decouple the prediction rate (here 2 Hz) from the execution rate (50 Hz: just step through a buffer), and they produce smoother, more consistent motion. Action chunking was popularised by ACT (Zhao et al., 2023) and is now standard in VLAs.

1. Full Model Pipeline

π0 is one transformer with two sets of weights, not two transformers. Every token is routed to exactly one weight set: images and text go to the VLM weights, robot state and actions go to the action-expert weights. In every layer, all tokens meet in a single self-attention. The paper describes the result as "more like a decoder-only mixture of experts".

Three things worth staring at:

  • The action expert reads the VLM at every depth. In layer ℓ the action tokens attend to the keys and values the VLM computed in the same layer ℓ. Contrast this with "cross-attend to the VLM's final output", which is how the π0 paper describes its smaller π0-small baseline, and how GR00T N1 (next module) works.
  • Two widths, one attention. The VLM is 2048 wide and the expert 1024 wide, but both project to the same 8 query heads × 256 dims and 1 KV head, so their tokens can sit in one softmax. Separate weights let the new robot-only inputs (state, noisy actions) use a smaller network, without disturbing the pretrained VLM weights.
  • The prefix is ~16× more tokens (816 vs 51) and ~6× more weights per layer than the suffix, yet it runs once per chunk while the suffix runs 10 times. The roofline section shows why the 10 cheap steps still cost real time on Jetson.

2. Flow Matching from Scratch

The diffusion module built DDPM. Flow matching is its simpler cousin: the same kind of network, simpler training and sampling math.

DDPMFlow matching (π0)
Training path for one (noise, data) pairSet by the noise schedule ᾱ_tStraight line between ε and a
Regression targetε (the noise)v = a − ε (the velocity of that line)
Extra mathSchedule ᾱ_t, variance bookkeepingLinear interpolation, MSE
Sampling1000 steps (DDPM) or ~50 (DDIM)~10 Euler steps
NetworkThe same kind of transformer; only the training target and the sampler change

A subtlety that trips people up: each training pair's path is straight, but the model does not know which data point a noisy xt came from. It learns the average velocity over all pairs that pass through xt. That averaged field curves, which is why we still need several Euler steps. With a single step, every sample is sent to the mean action. The widget below shows exactly that.

What to notice: with 1 step every sample jumps to x = 0, the average of "left" and "right", which is a bad action. With 2 steps samples split but many land between the modes. By ~10 steps nearly all land on a mode. With one mode, even 1 step works, because averaging one mode is harmless. That is the core argument for generative action heads over plain L2 regression.

Conventions: the same ODE written three ways

SourceNoisy pointTargetSampling
This guide, vla.py, GR00T's codext = (1−t)·ε + t·av = a − εstart at t = 0 (noise), x += v·dt, dt = +0.1
openpi (π0 / π0.5 code)xt = t·ε + (1−t)·au = ε − astart at t = 1 (noise), x += u·dt, dt = −0.1
π0 paper textAτ = τ·A + (1−τ)·εwritten as u = ε − A"from τ = 0 to τ = 1" with Aτ+δ = Aτ + δ·v

The first two are exactly equivalent: substitute topenpi = 1 − t and u = −v (the tests behind exercise 3 check it numerically). The paper's text pairs the first path with the second target's sign. If you port π0 weights or compare against openpi, follow the code, not the prose. All three agree on one practical detail: training times are not uniform. They are drawn from a Beta(1.5, 1)-based density that puts more samples at the noisy end, where predicting the velocity is hardest. In this guide's convention that is t = 0.999·(1 − u) with u ~ Beta(1.5, 1), so the mean t is 0.4.

3. Inside One Layer — Shared Attention

Why not simply freeze a VLM and bolt on a separate policy network? Because sharing attention at every layer lets the action tokens query intermediate visual and linguistic features, not just a final summary. And because the weights are separate, the pretrained VLM is not overwritten by inputs it never saw during web pretraining. π0 still trains both sides end-to-end. Section 6.2 covers the alternatives (freezing, "knowledge insulation").

4. Code Walkthrough

MoTLayer — two experts, one attention, a KV cache for free ▼ 
class MoTLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_vlm, d_act, n_heads, n_kv_heads, head_dim):
        self.vlm = ExpertBlock(d_vlm, n_heads, n_kv_heads, head_dim)   # prefix weights
        self.act = ExpertBlock(d_act, n_heads, n_kv_heads, head_dim)   # suffix weights

    def prefix(self, x):
        q, k, v = self.vlm.qkv(x)                       # prefix attends to prefix only
        return self.vlm.finish(x, attention(q, k, v, None)), (k, v)   # (k, v) = KV cache

    def suffix(self, y, kv_prefix, suffix_mask):
        q, k, v = self.act.qkv(y)
        k_all = torch.cat([kv_prefix[0], k], dim=2)       # [cached prefix keys ; new keys]
        v_all = torch.cat([kv_prefix[1], v], dim=2)
        mask = torch.cat([ones(T_s, T_p), suffix_mask], dim=1)   # suffix sees all prefix
        return self.act.finish(y, attention(q, k_all, v_all, mask))

Proof that the cache is exact: Pi0.forward_joint() runs one attention over the full 867-token sequence with the 3-block mask and no cache. python vla.py prints the maximum difference to the cached path (≈ 1e-6, float rounding). It is identical because no prefix row of the mask lets a prefix token see a suffix token.

compute_loss — one flow-matching training step ▼ 
def compute_loss(self, batch, time_dist="beta"):
    a = batch["action"]                                    # [B, H, action_dim]
    t = sample_flow_time(B, time_dist)                      # t = 0.999 * (1 - Beta(1.5, 1))
    eps = torch.randn_like(a)
    x_t = (1 - t) * eps + t * a                              # point on the straight line
    v_target = a - eps                                      # its constant velocity
    v_pred = self.velocity(batch["images"], batch["token_ids"], batch["state"], x_t, t)
    return F.mse_loss(v_pred, v_target)

Compared to DDPM training: no ᾱ table, no variance logic; the target is one subtraction. During training the prefix still runs once per sample. Only one flow time is drawn per sample, so there is nothing to cache.

generate_action — prefix once, 10 Euler steps ▼ 
@torch.no_grad()
def generate_action(self, images, token_ids, state, n_steps=10):
    cache = self.run_prefix(images, token_ids)                 # expensive: ONCE per chunk
    x = torch.randn(B, self.horizon, self.action_dim)          # t = 0: pure noise
    dt = 1.0 / n_steps
    for i in range(n_steps):
        t = torch.full((B,), i * dt)
        x = x + self.run_suffix(state, x, t, cache) * dt       # cheap: 51 tokens
    return x                                                   # [B, H, action_dim]

6. Critical Optimizations

6.1 Prefix KV cache (and why π0 uses 1 KV head) ▼

Without the cache, each Euler step would re-run SigLIP and all 18 Gemma-2B layers on 816 tokens. With it, a step processes 51 tokens and reads the cached prefix keys/values. For π0's shapes on an AGX Orin, the calculator's floor drops from ≈ 960 ms to ≈ 124 ms per chunk.

The cache is small because Gemma-2B uses multi-query attention (1 KV head): 18 layers × 2 (K, V) × 816 tokens × 256 dims × 2 bytes ≈ 15 MB. With 8 KV heads it would be 120 MB, read again at every Euler step.

6.2 Train everything, freeze, or insulate the VLM? ▼
  • π0 trains the whole model. The VLM starts from PaliGemma and the action expert from scratch. The paper describes no frozen components, and openpi's default fine-tuning configs update all weights (its "low_mem" variants switch to LoRA with a freeze filter).
  • Freezing the VLM (train only the expert and small adapters) is the cheap option: no VLM gradients or optimizer state, and the web-pretrained features cannot drift. GR00T N1.5 does this (next module).
  • Knowledge insulation (Driess et al., 2025): the authors found that naively adding a continuous action expert harms training speed and knowledge transfer. Their fix is to train the VLM backbone on discrete FAST action tokens and stop the expert's gradient from flowing into the VLM. The expert still produces continuous actions with flow matching at inference.
6.3 Precision: where quantization is safe ▼
ComponentStart withWhy
Vision encoder, VLM linear layersFP8 / INT8 weights (try FP4 on Thor)The bulk of the bytes and FLOPs; LLM-style weight quantization is mature
Action expertBF16 / FP16 firstSmall; its output is integrated over 10 steps, so errors accumulate
Action in/out projections, time embeddingFP16 / FP32Tiny, and they set the precision of the continuous output

Validate in closed loop: success rate on the robot, not just the MSE of the actions. For a published data point on a VLA-class model, see GR00T N1.7's Thor numbers in Section 7: a mixed NVFP4 TensorRT build runs a full policy call in 35.9 ms vs 77.6 ms, and NVIDIA checks it against a 0.99 cosine-similarity threshold on the final actions.

6.4 Fewer Euler steps ▼

π0 uses 10 steps; GR00T N1 uses 4. The 1-D widget shows the tradeoff: few steps pull samples toward the average of the modes. Halving the steps halves the action-expert time, which matters more on bandwidth-limited Jetsons than the FLOP count suggests. Going to 1–2 steps generally requires distillation-style training (an active research area). Re-evaluate task success whenever you change the step count.

6.5 Executing chunks: open loop, ensembling, real-time chunking ▼
  • Open loop with re-planning (π0): execute 25 of 50 actions on 50 Hz robots (16 on 20 Hz robots), then predict a new chunk. The π0 paper reports it chose not to aggregate overlapping chunks.
  • Temporal ensembling (ACT): predict every step and average the overlapping predictions for each timestep. Smoother, but needs inference at the control rate.
  • Real-time chunking (Black et al., 2025): compute the next chunk while executing the current one. The actions that will certainly execute during the inference delay are frozen, and the rest are "inpainted" so the new chunk joins smoothly. No retraining is needed for diffusion- or flow-based VLAs. GR00T N1.7's inference code ships an inpainting option of the same kind.

The budget rule: if inference takes Tinf and you execute e actions at f Hz between calls, you need Tinf < e / f to keep the robot moving without pauses (synchronous), or a method like RTC that tolerates the delay.

Measured numbers to calibrate against:

  • π0 on an RTX 4090 (paper, 3 cameras): 14 ms image encoders + 32 ms prefix + 27 ms for 10 action steps = 73 ms on-board.
  • GR00T N1.7 on Jetson (Isaac-GR00T deployment README, full policy call incl. preprocessing, 2 cameras at 256×256 → 156 VLM tokens, horizon 40, 4 denoising steps): AGX Thor (120 W) 111.5 ms in PyTorch eager, 77.6 ms with TensorRT, 35.9 ms with TensorRT + mixed NVFP4; Jetson Orin (30 W mode) 312.2 ms with TensorRT.
The roofline lesson. The prefix (816 tokens through ~2.4B weights) does ~800 FLOPs per byte, so it is compute-bound on Orin. Each Euler step pushes only 51 tokens through 0.3B weights, ~60 FLOPs per byte, so it is memory-bound everywhere. Ten such steps are under 10% of the FLOPs but about a quarter of the AGX Orin floor. To speed up the steps, shrink the bytes (quantize the expert and the KV cache, keep few KV heads) and the step count; faster tensor cores will not help.

Knobs for tighter budgets:

  1. Fewer cameras or fewer tokens per image (prefix cost is linear in tokens, plus a quadratic attention term)
  2. Lower-precision VLM weights (FP8/INT8, FP4 on Thor)
  3. Fewer Euler steps (10 → 5), re-validated on the task
  4. A smaller backbone: SmolVLA targets consumer GPUs and even CPUs and adds asynchronous inference
  5. Overlap inference with execution (real-time chunking) so latency stops being a hard stall

8. As of Oct 2026: What Came After π0

π0 (Oct 2024) is the clearest architecture to learn from. Physical Intelligence and others have shipped a lot since:
  • π0-FAST (Jan 2025): FAST compresses action chunks into discrete tokens with a discrete-cosine-transform-based scheme, so a VLM can predict actions autoregressively; PI reports it matches diffusion VLAs while cutting training time by up to 5×.
  • π0.5 (Apr 2025): pre-trains on discrete tokens only, then adds the flow-matching action expert in post-training. At inference it first predicts a high-level subtask in text, then the actions; it targets open-world generalization. In openpi, π0.5 injects flow time through adaptive RMSNorm instead of π0's action-time MLP.
  • Knowledge insulation (May 2025) and real-time chunking (Jun 2025): see 6.2 and 6.5.
  • π0.6 / π*0.6 (Nov 2025): π0.6 is built on a 5B-parameter VLM plus an action expert. π*0.6 adds "Recap" — RL from demonstrations, expert corrections and the robot's own experience, through a learned value function and advantage-conditioned policies. PI reports more than doubled throughput on some of the hardest tasks.
  • π0.7 (Apr 2026): a "steerable" generalist whose prompt can carry language, metadata (desired quality and speed), control-modality labels and visual subgoal images. PI reports early signs of compositional generalization. In Oct 2026 PI opened a Pi API beta for fine-tuning and hosted inference.
  • Elsewhere: OpenVLA-OFT (2025) showed that parallel decoding, action chunking, continuous actions and an L1 loss raise OpenVLA's LIBERO success from 76.5% to 97.1% and its action throughput 26×. Google DeepMind's Gemini Robotics (2025) and Gemini Robotics 1.5 pair a VLA with an embodied-reasoning model; its model page now lists a Gemini Robotics 2 family (VLA, ER 2, and On-Device 2). NVIDIA's GR00T N1.x line is the next module.

9. Connections

  • Robot foundation models (next module): GR00T N1 keeps the VLM + flow-matching action head recipe, but its DiT cross-attends to a middle VLM layer, and thin per-embodiment encoders/decoders let one model drive many robots.
  • BEV (previous module): robot VLAs feed per-camera ViT tokens straight into the VLM. Driving VLAs (Alpamayo) apply the same VLM + diffusion-decoder pattern to trajectories.
  • RL fine-tuning: flow-matching policies have no cheap exact log-likelihood, so standard policy-gradient recipes do not drop in directly. π*0.6's Recap sidesteps this by conditioning the policy on an advantage estimate instead.
  • World models: the flip side, p(next observation | action). π0.7's lightweight world model that generates visual subgoal images is one bridge between the two.

Key Takeaways

  • A VLA maps (images, instruction, robot state) to a chunk of H future actions. π0: H = 50, re-plan every 0.5 s on 50 Hz robots.
  • π0 is one transformer with two weight sets. Prefix tokens use the 3B VLM, state + action tokens use a 0.3B expert, and they meet in a shared attention at every layer.
  • The 3-block causal mask keeps the prefix blind to the suffix, so its KV cache is computed once and reused for all 10 Euler steps (exactly, not approximately).
  • Flow matching trains on straight lines xt = (1−t)ε + t·a with target a − ε, and samples with a few Euler steps. One step collapses to the mean action.
  • Conventions differ (openpi flips time and sign). Port from the code, not the prose.
  • On Jetson the prefix is compute-heavy, while the Euler steps are memory-bound: cut bytes (quantize, few KV heads) and steps.
  • As of Oct 2026, the π line moved to π0.5 → π0.6/π*0.6 (RL) → π0.7 (steerable prompts), with RTC for smooth real-time execution.

Self-Check

1. Why can π0 cache the prefix's keys and values across Euler steps, but not the suffix's?

Answer

The mask never lets a prefix token attend to a suffix token, so prefix activations (and their K/V) depend only on the images and text, which are fixed for the chunk. Suffix tokens contain xt and t, which change every step.

2. A 50 Hz robot executes 25 actions per chunk. What is the maximum inference latency that avoids a pause with synchronous execution, and what is the chunk prediction rate?

Answer

25 / 50 Hz = 0.5 s between calls, so inference must finish in under 500 ms; chunks are predicted at 2 Hz. (With π0's measured 73 ms on an RTX 4090 there is plenty of slack.)

3. With xt = (1−t)ε + t·a, what is dxt/dt, and why does a perfectly trained model's 1-step Euler sample equal the mean action?

Answer

dxt/dt = a − ε. At t = 0, x0 = ε carries no information about which a it will become, so the optimal prediction is E[a − ε | ε] = E[a] − ε. One Euler step gives x1 = ε + (E[a] − ε) = E[a] for every sample.

4. Convert openpi's (t = 0.7, u) to this guide's convention.

Answer

there = 1 − 0.7 = 0.3 and v = −u. The noisy point itself is the same: 0.7·ε + 0.3·a = (1 − 0.3)·ε + 0.3·a.

5. How big is π0's prefix KV cache for 3 cameras in BF16, and how big would it be with 8 KV heads instead of 1?

Answer

18 layers × 2 × 816 tokens × (1 × 256) × 2 bytes ≈ 15.0 MB. With 8 KV heads: 8× that, ≈ 120 MB, read at each of the 10 Euler steps.

6. What is the mean flow time under π0's training distribution (in this guide's convention), and why emphasize that end?

Answer

t = 0.999·(1 − u) with u ~ Beta(1.5, 1), so E[t] = 0.999 × (1 − 0.6) ≈ 0.40. Near t = 0 the input is almost pure noise and the velocity is hardest to predict (the multimodal averaging problem), so it gets more training samples.

7. Why does the roofline say 10 Euler steps cost about a quarter of the AGX Orin floor although they are under 10% of the FLOPs?

Answer

Each step does ~38 GFLOPs but reads ~0.64 GB (expert weights + KV cache), about 60 FLOPs per byte, far below Orin's ridge point (~200). So each step is limited by 204.8 GB/s: ≈ 3.1 ms, ≈ 31 ms for 10 steps, out of a 124 ms floor.

8. π0 vs "freeze the VLM and cross-attend to its final output": name one advantage of each.

Answer

π0's per-layer shared attention gives the action tokens access to intermediate features at every depth and trains end-to-end. Freezing plus cross-attention is cheaper to fine-tune, protects the VLM's pretrained knowledge, and lets you quantize the frozen VLM without worrying about gradients.

Hands-On Exercises

Run from the vla/ folder. All run on CPU in seconds.

1. Verify the cache

Build vla_small(), random inputs with 2 cameras, and check velocity() against forward_joint(). Then break the mask (allow prefix rows to see the suffix in forward_joint) and confirm they now differ.

assert torch.allclose(m.velocity(*args), m.forward_joint(*args), atol=1e-5)

2. Steps vs modes

With gaussian_mixture_velocity and euler_integrate, measure the fraction of 20,000 samples landing within 0.3 of ±1 for 1, 2, 4, 10 steps. You should see 0%, ~39%, ~92%, ~99%. Then move the modes to ±3 and see how many steps you need now.

3. Prove the conventions agree

Integrate the same initial noise with this guide's sampler (t: 0 → 1, v) and with an openpi-style loop (s: 1 → 0, u(x, s) = −v(x, 1 − s), dt = −0.1). assert torch.allclose on the results.

4. Train a tiny flow policy

Train a 3-layer MLP vθ(x, t) on the bimodal 1-D data with the flow-matching loss (use sample_flow_time). After ~2,000 Adam steps, sample with 10 Euler steps and check that more than 90% of samples land within 0.3 of a mode. Then train a plain regressor a = f(noise) with MSE and observe it predicts ≈ 0.
